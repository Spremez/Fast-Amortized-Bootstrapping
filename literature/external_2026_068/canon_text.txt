     Abstract. Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) enables computation on encrypted
     data. NTRU-based FHE offers compact ciphertexts—a single ring element rather
     than a tuple—but FINAL, the leading NTRU-based scheme, represents its ciphertexts
     and decryption in matrix form that hides the polynomial-ring structure on which
     recent automorphism-based amortized bootstrapping relies.
     We reformulate FINAL in the standard polynomial-ring setting and express its decryp-
     tion as coefficient-wise inner products compatible with FHEW-style accumulators.
     This preserves the required ring automorphisms and lets us adapt monomial-by-
     polynomial amortized bootstrapping to NTRU ciphertexts with sparse secret keys:
     for a secret of Hamming weight h, the dominant per-coefficient work drops from
     O(nℓQ ) to O(hℓpos ) external-product operations, where ℓQ and ℓpos denote the gadget-
     decomposition lengths of the standard and position-based bootstrapping keys, re-
     spectively. Concrete parameters are selected by a joint optimization that combines a
     refined average-case noise analysis with security validation via NTRU fatigue analysis
     and the Lattice Estimator under sparse-secret distributions.
     Our highly optimized single-threaded C++ implementation based on Intel HEXL
     bootstraps a message coefficient in 2.68 ms at n = 8192—45× faster than FINAL and
     2.6× faster than TFHE-rs. The bootstrapping key does not scale with n (11.28 MB
     at n = 8192). Packed ciphertexts cost about 8 bytes per message bit, and the total
     key material remains below that of state-of-the-art amortized RLWE bootstrapping.
     Keywords: Fully Homomorphic Encryption · NTRU · Amortized Bootstrapping


1    Introduction
Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) enables computation directly on encrypted data,
but bootstrapping remains a major performance bottleneck—particularly in FHEW-
style bitwise schemes, where it refreshes ciphertexts and evaluates nonlinear operations.
Therefore, reducing the cost and memory footprint of bootstrapping is therefore a central
challenge in designing practical FHE schemes.
    NTRU-based FHE is attractive because its ciphertexts are compact: a single ring
element rather than a tuple of them. Early constructions such as LTV [LTV12] and
BLLN [BLLN13] were later broken in the overstretched parameter regime [ABD16, KF17],
but Ducas and van Woerden showed how to avoid this regime [DvW21], and Bonte
et al. [BIP+ 22] subsequently demonstrated FINAL, an NTRU-based FHE scheme with
competitive bootstrapping performance under conservative parameters.
    The key idea behind FINAL is a matrix-NTRU formulation that expresses decryption as
an inner product, which can be evaluated homomorphically inside an FHEW-style accumu-
lator. This advantage comes at a structural cost: monomial-by-polynomial (MPMul) amor-
tized bootstrapping packs n messages into a single accumulator polynomial and traverses
the secret key through monomial multiplications and Galois automorphisms—inherently
2                              Practical Amortized Bootstrapping for NTRU-Based FHE


ring operations with no natural counterpart once ciphertexts are treated as matrices and
vectors over Zq . FINAL therefore retains compact NTRU ciphertexts but cannot directly
benefit from automorphism-based amortization.
    Can we obtain an NTRU-based FHE scheme that simultaneously preserves compact
NTRU ciphertexts, supports FHEW-style bootstrapping, and enables amortized bootstrap-
ping via polynomial automorphisms? Returning to the standard polynomial formulation of
NTRU [HPS98], we decompose the decryption product into coefficient-wise inner products.
This decomposition plays the same role as FINAL’s matrix lifting—making decryption
compatible with FHEW-style accumulators—while operating natively within the ring.
This ensures that ciphertexts and secret keys are kept as ring elements, leaving the
automorphisms required for amortization fully accessible.
    Building on this observation, we adapt the MPMul-based amortized bootstrapping to
NTRU ciphertext. Guimarães and Pereira [GP25] originally developed this technique for
RLWE ciphertext. The adaptation is nontrivial—NTRU accumulator ciphertexts are scalar
ring elements of the form g/f rather than RLWE tuples, and accumulator operations must
preserve this structure—and is achieved through a difference-vector representation of the
sparse secret-key positions and an NTRU-compatible MPMul procedure for accumulator
ciphertexts. For a sparse ternary secret of Hamming weight h, the procedure traverses
only the h nonzero key positions instead of all n, reducing the amortized cost per refreshed
coefficient from O(n ℓQ ) to O(h ℓpos )—where ℓpos = O(log Rpos ) is the short gadget length
induced by the bound Rpos on the gaps between nonzero positions—and shrinking the
dominant bootstrapping-key material to sparse position-dependent keys. We further
shorten all gadget lengths via an approximate gadget decomposition, and derive concrete
parameters via a joint optimization that combines a refined average-case noise analysis
with a failure-probability target of 2−64 ; security is validated against the NTRU fatigue
analysis and the Lattice Estimator for sparse secrets.
    We benchmark an optimized C++ implementation, accelerated with Intel HEXL and
executed single-threaded on identical hardware, against the reference implementation
of FINAL [BIP+ 22], the highly optimized TFHE-rs library [Zam22], and the amortized
bootstrapping of Guimarães and Pereira [GP25]. The amortized cost per refreshed message
decreases monotonically with the packing dimension, from 5.02 ms at n = 1024 to 2.68 ms
at n = 8192—speed-ups of 24.14× to 45.22× over FINAL and of 1.41× to 2.65× over
TFHE-rs—while staying within 1.35× (n = 2048) to 1.17× (n = 8192) of GP25, with the
gap narrowing as n grows. Our key material is strictly smaller: the bootstrapping key
does not scale with n and shrinks to 11.28 MB at n = 8192—7.7× below FINAL’s and
consistently below GP25’s—the total key size is up to 1.98× smaller than GP25’s, and
packed ciphertexts cost about 8 bytes per message bit, half of GP25’s per-slot footprint
and roughly a 400× density improvement over single-bit FHE schemes.
    In summary, our main contributions are as follows:

    • An accumulator-compatible reformulation of NTRU decryption as coefficient-
      wise inner products, showing that FHEW-style bootstrapping is directly compatible
      with the standard polynomial formulation of NTRU—without FINAL’s matrix lifting,
      and while preserving the ring automorphisms required for amortization.

    • An amortized bootstrapping procedure for NTRU ciphertexts with sparse
      ternary keys, combining an NTRU-compatible MPMul with a difference-vector
      encoding of secret-key positions, reducing the per-coefficient cost from O(n ℓQ ) to
      O(h ℓpos ) and correspondingly shrinks the bootstrapping key.

    • A refined noise analysis and joint parameter optimization with approximate
      gadget decompositions under a failure-probability target. Security is validated using
      NTRU fatigue analysis and the Lattice Estimator, together with an optimized
W.T. Lin and J.L. Wu                                                                                   3


       C++/HEXL implementation evaluated single-threaded against FINAL, TFHE-rs,
       and GP25 on identical hardware.

2.3     FHEW-Style Bootstrapping
FHEW-style bootstrapping [DM15, CGGI16] refreshes a noisy ciphertext ct ∈ Rq by
homomorphically evaluating its decryption inside an accumulator: ct is lifted into an
accumulator ciphertext over R  b Q initialized with a test polynomial, the accumulator is
blind-rotated using a bootstrapping key bsk. Because bsk encrypts the base secret key under
the accumulator scheme, the refreshed message is extracted—typically as the constant
term of the resulting polynomial—and modulus- and key-switching convert the result
back to the base key. We follow this paradigm, but instantiate both the base and the
accumulator scheme with NTRU ciphertexts; the realization of blind rotation via NTRU
external products is presented in Section 3.3.


3     Polynomial NTRU FHE Scheme
FINAL [BIP+ 22] expresses decryption as an inner product between a ciphertext and the
first column col0 (F−1 ) of the inverse secret-key matrix. This representation is well suited
to accumulator-based bootstrapping, but Galois automorphisms and monomial shifts—the
operations on which MPMul-based amortization relies—have no natural counterpart in
this matrix–vector form. We therefore restate the relevant NTRU operations directly over
the polynomial ring: the reformulation preserves the decryption structure required by
the accumulator while supporting the automorphism and monomial interface used by the
amortized procedure of Section 4.

3.1     Polynomial-Ring NTRU Base Scheme

scheme follows the NTRU encryption structure underlying FINAL [BIP+ 22] and represents

message in Z4 and use the scaling factor ∆ := ⌊q/4⌉, whereas the outputs of homomorphic
NAND gates carry a message in Z2 and therefore use ∆ := ⌊q/2⌉.

4     Amortized Bootstrapping for NTRU-Based FHE
4.1     Amortized Bootstrapping via Sparse-Key MPMul
The MPMul-based amortization of Guimarães and Pereira [GP25] was developed for RLWE,
where ciphertexts are tuples of ring elements; NTRU accumulator ciphertexts are scalar
ring elements of the form g/f , so the required monomial operations must be realized
while preserving this structure. Beyond the decryption reformulation in Section 3.2, our
construction uses three components: sparse-key polynomial multiplication, sign selection
for ternary coefficients, and an automorphism key switching step that maps ciphertexts
associated with fQ (X −1 ) back to fQ (X). A dense coefficient-wise evaluation would
require n2 external accumulator for the n outputs of a packed ciphertext; the amortized
construction instead uses h MPMul invocations, where h is the Hamming weight of the
sparse key.

5     Security and Noise Analysis
5.1    Security Model and Assumptions
IND-CPA Security The security of both encryption schemes and all auxiliary keys reduces
to the decisional NTRU assumption over their respective rings Rq and R̂Q . Under this
assumption, fresh ciphertexts—including the base scheme c = (g + ∆ · m) · f −1 and the
accumulator forms ACC.EncS and ACC.EncV—are computationally indistinguishable from
uniform samples due to randomization with fresh NTRU-distributed noise terms. Similarly,
the pseudorandomness of all auxiliary keys is guaranteed by the same encryption scheme,
under the standard circular-security assumption required for bootstrapping.


Circular Security Bootstrapping additionally publishes encryptions of key material under
related keys (e.g., BSKrot and kskext ), as in FHEW [DM15], TFHE [CGGI16], and FI-
NAL [BIP+ 22], we assume weak circular security: these key-dependent encryptions remain
indistinguishable from encryptions of independent messages. No known attacks exploit
this circular structure in NTRU-based schemes.


5.2    Avoiding overstretched NTRU
For concrete security, we bound the admissible ciphertext moduli using the NTRU Fatigue
script by Ducas and van Woerden [DvW21], which verifies parameters against sublattice
attacks. We set the key-distribution variance to σ 2 = 0.5, corresponding to our ternary
key distribution with Pr[X = 0] = 12 and Pr[X = 1] = Pr[X = −1] = 14 . The NTRU type
is set to circulant in the script to distinguish it from the matrix variant; we argue that the
anti-circulant ring used in our scheme does not weaken security relative to the circulant
case considered in the script.
    To convert a BKZ block size β into a security level, we adopt the same classical cost
model used by TFHE [CGGI16] and FINAL [BIP+ 22]: a security level of λ bits requires
T (d, β) ≥ 2λ , where T (d, β) := 20.292·β+16.4+log2 (8·d) , and d = 2n for an NTRU ciphertext
in dimension n. We write β128 for the smallest block size such that T (d, β128 ) ≥ 2128 at
the respective lattice dimension.
    Rather than fixing the moduli in advance, we use the script to determine, for each
candidate ring dimension, the largest modulus that remains outside the overstretched
regime and still yields a predicted attack block size that meets β128 . Table 4 reports these
boundaries, quantized to the largest NTT-friendly primes supported by our implementation,
along with β128 and the corresponding certified level λ = log2 T (d, β128 ). The boundary
at dimension n upper-bounds the base modulus q, and the boundary at dimension N
upper-bounds the accumulator modulus Q; the parameter selection in Section 6.2 draws
moduli only from within these limits.
    Since every admissible modulus lies outside the overstretched regime, sublattice attacks
are no easier than generic key-recovery attacks on lattice-based schemes, and the BKZ-
based bound above provides a meaningful estimate of the classical security level. The
Hamming weight h of the sparse secret introduces an additional attack surface, which we
evaluate separately in the next subsection using the Lattice Estimator [DvW21].

5.3    Sparse-Key Security
In amortized bootstrapping, we use a secret key with Hamming weight h, which is
known to be vulnerable to the dual hybrid attack with meet-in-the-middle optimiza-
tion [CHHS19]. We therefore evaluate every candidate key configuration with the Lattice
Estimator [DvW21]: for each candidate Hamming weight h and each candidate base mod-
ulus q below the corresponding boundary in Table 4, we denote by λ0 (h, q) the resulting
security level, defined as the minimum rop value across all attacks supported       by the
estimator. Throughout this analysis, the noise distribution is fixed at σ = 1/2 ≈ 0.707.
                                                                             p

    The per-message cost of amortized bootstrapping scales with both h and ℓpos =
⌈log2 Rpos ⌉, so small values of both are preferable. However, the rejection sampling used
to enforce the position-bound Rpos conditions the key distribution, thereby compromising
security. We therefore define the effective security of a candidate (h, q, Rpos ) as λ :=
λ0 (h, q) − δ, where δ is the rejection-induced loss quantified below. We deem a candidate
admissible only if λ ≥ 128. The joint selection of (h, Rpos ) and the remaining scheme
parameters over this admissible space is deferred to the runtime-driven optimization in
Section 6.2.

Rejection Sampling To reduce the per-step cost of the external product, we apply
rejection sampling during key generation and compute the corresponding security loss
following the methodology of Guimarães and Pereira [GP25]. Let d := diff(f ) denote the
sequence of gaps between consecutive non-zero positions of the sparse secret f , and let
Rpos be an upper bound on these gaps. During key generation, we accept a candidate key
only if |d[i]| < Rpos , for all 0 ≤ i < h.
    To quantify the impact of this restriction on the key distribution, we sample a large
number of random keys (e.g., 105 ), compute M (f ) := max0≤i<h |d[i]|, and estimate the
acceptance probability as pacc (Rpos ) := Pr[M (f ) < Rpos ]. The security loss induced by
rejection sampling is then defined as δ := − log2 pacc (Rpos ).
    Equivalently, the rejection-conditioned key distribution loses approximately δ bits of
security compared with uniform sampling over the original sparse-key support. We discard
candidate (h, Rpos ) pairs with low acceptance probability because NTRU key generation
independently rejects non-invertible candidates and a low acceptance rate would compound
the two rejection conditions, making sampling impractical.

the optimization: the estimator output λ0 (at the selected base modulus q), the acceptance

6     Implementation and Evaluation
6.1     Implementation
We implemented the proposed polynomial-NTRU FHE scheme with sparse-key amortized
bootstrapping in C++. Polynomial multiplication dominates both external products and
key switching, so we accelerate these operations using Intel HEXL [BKS+ 21] for NTT-based
multiplication and coefficient-wise modular arithmetic. As required by HEXL, we restrict
the ring dimensions to powers of two and choose moduli such that q ≡ 1 (mod 2n) and
Q ≡ 1 (mod 2N ); this NTT-friendliness constraint is already reflected in the modulus
boundaries of Table 4.

Approximate Gadget Decomposition An external product with decomposition length ℓ
requires ℓ+1 transforms—ℓ forward NTTs for the digit polynomials plus one inverse NTT—
so ℓ directly scales the dominant cost of bootstrapping. To shorten the decomposition
beyond what an exact gadget allows, we also support an approximate signed gadget: for a
power-of-two base B = 2b and modulus M , the least significant drop bits of the input are
rounded away before decomposition, leaving ℓ = ⌈(⌈log2 M ⌉ − drop)/b⌉ digits instead of
⌈logB M ⌉. For instance, reducing ℓQ from 2 to 1 removes one-third of the transforms for
each external product.
    The rounding introduces an error ε uniformly distributed on (−2drop−1 , 2drop−1 ], so
Var(ε) = 4drop /12. In an external product, this error is multiplied by the scaled accumulator
secret fQ = 1+4fQ  ′

    The search proceeds in two levels. The outer level iterates over the admissible space
(N, q, Q, h, ℓpos ). For each such tuple, the correctness target is converted to a noise budget
Eout ≤ Emax (q) using the complementary error function, and the inner level allocates this
budget across the three gadgets: for each gadget and each achievable length ℓ we precompute
the noise-minimal pair (B, drop)—a Pareto frontier over bases and drop amounts—and
then scan the combinations of lengths, keeping the cheapest one whose total noise fits the
budget. Approximate gadgets are thus selected automatically whenever the transforms
they save outweigh the noise they introduce. Table 6 reports the resulting parameter sets;
by construction, each set provides at least 128-bit classical security and a per-slot failure
probability below 2−64 . Notably, the search converges to a single accumulator configuration
(N, Q) = (1024, 925,697)—the boundary modulus of Table 4—for all four base dimensions,
and uses the approximate gadget only on the base-ring side (dropQ = dropaut = 0).
Discarding 5–7 low-weight bits shortens ℓq to as little as 4 and, as a side effect, shrinks
the external key-switching key.

6.4    Performance
Table 7 reports the bootstrapping performance of our scheme against these three baselines.
Each entry reports average wall-clock times measured over 100 runs: per-batch latency
and amortized time per bootstrapped message coefficient. For amortized schemes, the
latter equals the per-batch time divided by the number of refreshed messages; for FINAL
and TFHE, which encrypt a single bit per ciphertext, the two coincide.
    The per-coefficient cost of the proposed scheme decreases monotonically with the
packing dimension, from 5.02 ms at n = 1024 to 2.68 ms at n = 8192, consistent with the
O(hℓpos ) per-coefficient complexity established in Section 4.3. The admissible Hamming
weight h shrinks as n grows, so amortization improves with the packing factor. A single
accumulator dimension N = 1024 suffices across all parameter sets.
    On a per-coefficient basis, the proposed scheme outperforms FINAL by 24.14× to
45.22× and the highly optimized TFHE baseline (7.10 ms per bit) by 1.41× to 2.65×, while
each packed ciphertext additionally carries n message bits. Compared with GP25, our
per-coefficient cost is 1.17× to 1.35× higher, with the gap narrowing as n grows. We note
that GP25 also relies on a two-key accumulator technique that our implementation does not
incorporate; whether and to what extent this technique applies to our polynomial-NTRU
setting is left for future work.

7    Conclusion and Future Work
We revisited NTRU-based FHE, motivated by the refined security analysis of Ducas and van
Woerden [DvW21] and the FINAL construction of Bonte et al. [BIP+ 22]. Reformulating
FINAL’s matrix-based construction in the standard polynomial-ring setting restores
the algebraic structure required for automorphism-based amortization; combining an
NTRU-compatible MPMul with sparse ternary secrets then reduces the per-coefficient
bootstrapping cost from O(nℓQ ) to O(hℓpos ) and corresponding bootstrapping-key size.
We further shortens the gadget length through an approximate base-ring decomposition.
Concrete security is established by a two-step parameter selection procedure that combines
the NTRU fatigue analysis with the Lattice Estimator under a given failure probability
target.
    Our single-threaded C++/HEXL implementation refreshes a message coefficient in
2.68 ms at n = 8192—up to 45× faster than FINAL and 2.65× faster than TFHE-rs—with
an 11.28 MB bootstrapping key that does not grow with n and packed ciphertexts of about
8 bytes per message bit. These results establish polynomial-NTRU FHE as a competitive
alternative to mainstream bitwise schemes and the more economical option in key- and
bandwidth-constrained deployments.
    The most immediate open question is whether GP25’s two-key accumulator technique
applies to the polynomial-NTRU setting, which would close the remaining 1.17× to 1.35×
runtime gap. Beyond this, compressing the key-switching key—now the dominant part of
the total footprint—and exploiting multi-threading or GPU acceleration are natural next
steps.
